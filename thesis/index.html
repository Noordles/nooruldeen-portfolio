The longer economic picture

I keep the history from 2003 and compare the yearly figures as they were published.

How to read this view
  • Real GDP per person: economic output divided by population, adjusted to 2015 prices. It is not household income.
  • Real GDP growth: the annual percentage change in economic output after price changes.
  • Manufacturing share: manufacturing value added as a percentage of GDP.
  • Oil rents: the estimated value of oil production after production costs, as a percentage of GDP. This is not government oil revenue.

Each point is a published annual observation. Missing years stay missing. Hover or tap a line to inspect the country, year and saved value.

Select country lines to focus on them. Select several to compare, or show only your selection.

Source-backed context in this period

Does a more complex model help?

I compare five methods on the same 24 monthly total-revenue increments, from July 2024 to June 2026, in nominal trillion IQD. The models do not forecast the monthly budget balance. A three-month horizon predicts the single third target month, not a three-month sum. MAE is the average size of the errors; RMSE gives bigger mistakes more weight.

How to read the forecast comparison
  • MAE: average absolute prediction error. Lower is better.
  • RMSE: prediction error with more weight given to large mistakes. Lower is better.
  • Trillion IQD: one trillion Iraqi dinars, at the prices of that period.
  • 95% prediction interval: the model’s uncertainty range for a future observation, under its assumptions.

The methods share 19 origins in each evaluation stage. Selection uses earlier validation MAE; held-out results are reported separately. Multi-month errors can be dependent. The chart uses a saved June 2026 origin.

WHAT I FOUND SO FAR AR(1) has the lowest MAE. ARIMA(1,1,1) has the lowest RMSE. I still need to check the model assumptions and remaining patterns in the errors before making my final choice.

The saved origin ends in June 2026; it is not a fresh forecast. Shaded ranges are conditional model intervals. Simple benchmarks have no supplied intervals, which does not mean zero uncertainty.

What if oil prices fall?

I use the IMF's April 2026 yearly projections as a starting point. Change the assumptions to see how the budget balance changes.

How to read the budget scenario
  • Baseline: the balance calculated from the IMF’s published annual revenue, spending and GDP projections.
  • Scenario: the balance after your price assumption changes the selected share of revenue.
  • Balance as % of GDP: revenue minus spending, divided by fixed baseline GDP. Negative values show a deficit; positive values show a surplus.
  • Percentage points: the difference between the two balance percentages, not a percentage change.

The sensitive share is an assumption. Price and quantity multiply together; other revenue and spending have separate controls. GDP and the exchange rate remain fixed.

The assumptions describe an accounting scenario, not its likelihood. The balancing threshold solves the same equation; it does not predict a market oil price.

Selected annual money flow

Four completed CBI years, 2022–2025. The 2024 and 2025 totals use December cumulative accounts. These preliminary, revisable figures stay separate from the IMF perimeter and projections.

The published spending breakdown

What makes a country comparable?

I compare countries using their 2003–2007 oil rents, real GDP per person and manufacturing. Change how much each feature matters to see how the ranking changes. My calculation uses log GDP per person to reduce the effect of very large income differences.

How to read the country comparison

Iraq is the reference, with a distance of zero. Every loaded comparison country is listed. The features come from the 2003–2007 period.

  • Weights: how much each feature matters after the features are put on a common scale. Zero excludes that feature.
  • Income: log real GDP per person, which reduces the effect of very large income differences.
  • Distance: a unitless similarity measure. A lower value means a closer match on the selected features.

The ranking describes similarity within this country set. It does not establish why economies developed differently.
